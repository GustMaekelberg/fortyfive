## 1. Market size — cyclists (regular cyclists, commuters, e-bike owners, cycling in the dark, bike/e-bike sales) in Belgium/Flanders and the Netherlands

### Takeaway
The cyclist base is very large and electrifying fast: Belgium sold 578,737 bikes in 2025 (+7.1%, counter to the European trend), 45% of Flemish households own an e-bike, 55% of Flemings cycle at least weekly and ~22% of Flemish commute trips are by bike (half of those electric); the Netherlands has ~12.8 million cyclists and ~4.2 million e-bikes but a shrinking new-bike market (795,968 units in 2025, lowest in ~40 years). Only a minority of cycling distance is ridden in darkness (~10% in an older Dutch study), so the relevant sub-segment is winter commuters, not "all cyclists".

### Inferences
- Implied Belgian 2024 total ≈ 540,000 bikes (578,737 / 1.071). Belgium is one of the few growing bike markets in Europe in 2025–2026, largely lease-driven; the Netherlands is larger in installed base but contracting in new sales.
- Flanders: 29% daily + 26% weekly cyclists = 55% cycling at least weekly. With a Flemish population of roughly 6.8 million (general knowledge, not sourced in this research), that is on the order of 3.5–3.7 million weekly cyclists and ~2 million daily cyclists (upper bound; the survey sample base may be adults only).
- Bike commuters are the relevant core: 22% of Flemish commute trips by bike, 11% by e-bike. In December/January in Belgium/NL both legs of a standard working day fall largely in darkness or twilight (general knowledge), so commuters are the people who actually meet oncoming headlights daily for ~3–4 months per year — but the SWOV figure (~10% of distance in darkness) warns that the year-round exposure of the average cyclist is small.
- NL: 43% of ~12.8 million cyclists using an e-bike implies roughly 5.5 million e-bike users in 2025 (consistent with the 4.2 million e-bikes estimated for 2024).
- The Dutch average-price figures in the snippets are inconsistent: €1.53 bn / 795,968 units implies ≈ €1,920 per bike, whereas snippets also mention "average just under €1,200" (probably a different delimitation, e.g. non-electric bikes or a median). Verify before quoting.

## 2. Market size — speed pedelecs (registrations, fleet, Flanders share, rider profile, spend, trends 2024–2026)

### Takeaway
Belgium is a uniquely concentrated speed pedelec market: a record 23,681 registrations in 2025 (14,058 new + 9,623 used), ~97.5–97.9% of them in Flanders, a sector-estimated fleet of "almost 150,000", and first-half 2026 again up strongly (8,196 new + 6,582 used). The Netherlands is 4–5x smaller and stagnating (35,415 on the road on 1 Jan 2025; only ~3,100 new in 2024; owners average 53 years old). The Belgian rider is predominantly a commuter on an employer-leased bike costing €3,000–10,000+.

### Inferences
- Implied Belgian new registrations: 2024 ≈ 13,700 (14,058 / 1.026); 2023 ≈ 16,400–16,500 (if 2024 was "more than a sixth" lower); used 2024 ≈ 7,760 (9,623 / 1.24). So roughly 44,000 new speed pedelecs entered Flanders in 2023–2025 alone, plus ~8,200 in H1 2026.
- Belgium vs Netherlands: new sales ~13,700 (BE, 2024) vs ~3,100 (NL, 2024) = ~4.4x; fleet ~150,000 (BE sector estimate) vs 35,415 (NL) = ~4.2x — with under two-thirds of the Dutch population. Practically the whole Belgian market (~146,000 bikes if the 150,000 estimate holds) sits in Flanders, i.e. a single-language, geographically compact target that a small Dutch-language webshop can reach.
- Rider profiles differ sharply: Belgium = employee on a lease bike (62–66% of new registrations via leasing companies; cafeteria-plan/bike-lease mechanics, recently extended to education and military staff), predominantly commuting; Netherlands = private owner (90%), average age 53, half aged 55+. Older eyes are more glare-sensitive (general knowledge), so the Dutch owner base may be individually more receptive, but it is much smaller and not growing.
- Illustrative reach only (not a forecast): 1% / 3% / 5% penetration of a ~150,000-rider Flemish fleet = 1,500 / 4,500 / 7,500 buyers; the annual inflow of new riders (new + first-time used buyers, roughly 15,000–24,000 registrations a year) is the recurring pool.
- A rider who commutes 20–40 km each way at up to 45 km/h on roads/cycle paths alongside car traffic has both higher exposure to oncoming headlights and more to lose from 1–2 seconds of reduced vision than a city cyclist; they already must wear a helmet (often with a visor) and are used to buying gear.
- The used boom (ex-lease bikes, +24% in 2025, +48% in H1 2026) broadens the segment toward more price-sensitive private buyers.

## 4. Demand signals — do cyclists in BE/NL experience glare, complain about it and look for a solution? Search interest and seasonality

### Takeaway

### Inferences
- Visors are a spare-part business tied to each helmet's mount; a third-party "anti-glare visor" would need a separate SKU per helmet model (ABUS Pedelec 2.0 ACE, HYP-E, Cratoni Commuter/Smartride, Lazer Anverz, R2B, ...) and would compete with OEM spares at €44–71. Glasses (or a universal clip-on/fit-over element) are brand-agnostic and far simpler for a small webshop.
- OEM visor tints are clear, smoke or photochromic — none is marketed for headlight glare. That is a visible product gap, but also a hint that helmet brands (who face certification and liability) have not seen a defensible claim to make.
- Because a visor-equipped helmet already protects the eyes from wind and rain, a speed pedelec rider with a visor has less reason to wear separate glasses; the glasses product then has to fit *under* a visor/helmet or replace the visor's function.
- No specialist "speed pedelec accessories" webshop surfaced in Belgium or the Netherlands; the niche appears unoccupied as a retail concept, although the search was limited (see gaps).

## 7. Earlier start-ups, Kickstarters and dropshipping "night vision glasses" brands — reputation and consumer-organisation warnings

### Takeaway
No start-up or crowdfunding campaign built around anti-headlight-glare eyewear or visors *for cyclists* was found; the driver-oriented category is dominated by dropshipping brands with scam-alert coverage and refund complaints, and independent consumer bodies and researchers (Stiftung Warentest, ADAC, Which?, a 2019 Harvard/Schepens study) advise against yellow night-driving glasses. No Test-Aankoop or Consumentenbond test of nachtbrillen was found.

**Consumer organisations, motoring clubs, science**
- Stiftung Warentest ("Nachtfahrbrillen: Experten raten ab"): tinted lenses improved neither twilight acuity nor glare sensitivity and often made vision worse; experts advise against. — [test.de](https://www.test.de/Nachtfahrbrillen-Experten-raten-ab-1069128-0/)
- ADAC (ophthalmologist Prof. Bernhard Lachenmayr): yellow-filter "night driving glasses" are not suitable for drivers, can increase glare sensitivity and worsen twilight vision; recommended alternative is untinted, fully anti-reflective lenses. — [ADAC](https://www.adac.de/gesundheit/krankheiten/nachtblindheit/); [auto motor und sport](https://www.auto-motor-und-sport.de/verkehr/nachts-geblendet-sicherheitsrisko-spezialbrillen/)
- Which? ("Are night driving glasses safe to wear?"): yellow-lens glasses are touted to reduce headlight dazzle, but experts say they are not a good idea. — [Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/are-night-driving-glasses-safe-to-wear-a20Q17g8xnyD)
- What Car? road-tested six pairs on a 12-mile lit/unlit route; a clinical adviser stated there is no strong evidence that blue-blocking or yellow-tinted lenses reduce glare. — [What Car?](https://www.whatcar.com/news/anti-glare-night-driving-glasses-review/n28823)
- 2019 Schepens Eye Research Institute/Harvard driving-simulator study: yellow-lens glasses did not improve pedestrian detection at night nor mitigate headlight glare; any tint removes light, "similar to driving in sunglasses". — [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20190802/Yellow-lens-glasses-do-not-improve-night-driving-visibility-study-finds.aspx); [Medical Xpress](https://m.medicalxpress.com/news/2019-08-night-yellow-lenses-wont.html); [Review of Optometry](https://www.reviewofoptometry.com/news/article/yellow-lenses-no-help-in-night-driving)
- WebMD: research suggests night driving glasses may make night vision worse; All About Vision: "help or hoax?"; AAO EyeNet covers night-driving difficulties. — [WebMD](https://www.webmd.com/eye-health/cm/what-to-know-about-night-driving-glasses); [All About Vision](https://www.allaboutvision.com/night-driving-glasses/); [AAO EyeNet](https://www.aao.org/eyenet/article/driving-at-night)
- Belgium/Netherlands: Test-Aankoop labelled the Horizane anti-blue-light glasses (≈ €25–30) a "Pinokkio product" with no proven benefit and separately advised against Blacksheep glasses — showing it does police eyewear claims; no nachtbril test by Test-Aankoop or Consumentenbond was found. — [Test-Aankoop Horizane](https://www.test-aankoop.be/gezond/pinokkioproducten/antiblauwlichtbril-horizane); [Test-Aankoop Blacksheep](https://www.test-aankoop.be/gezond/gezondheidszorg/medische-hulpmiddelen/nieuws/brillen-blacksheep-test)
- In contrast, ANWB's dazzle advice mentions yellow-lens glasses as an option and its shop sells them. — [ANWB verblinding](https://www.anwb.nl/auto/rijden/verblinding)

### Inferences
- The category's public reputation is poor in exactly the places a quality-minded buyer will look (Stiftung Warentest, ADAC, Which?, mainstream health sites). A webshop making "blocks headlight glare" claims risks (a) being lumped in with dropshippers, (b) attention from Test-Aankoop/Radar-type watchdogs, which already report on eyewear with unproven claims, and (c) high return rates ("no noticeable effect").
- The absence of any cyclist-specific predecessor cuts both ways: an unoccupied niche, or one that others judged not viable/defensible.
- Dropshippers' modus operandi (advertorial pages, fake-urgency discounts, multi-pair bundles, weak refunds) defines what a credible entrant must visibly *not* do: local returns address, transparent pricing, modest and substantiated claims.

### Gaps
- The specific brands named in the brief ("ClearView", "Night Sight", "HD Vision Night") did not surface with documented outcomes; the planned search on advertising-regulator rulings (ASA/FTC) could not be run.
- No Radar/Kassa item specifically about nachtbrillen and no Dutch/Belgian press piece with an ophthalmologist's verdict was retrieved (search could not be run). Full text and dates of the Which?, test.de and ADAC pieces were not readable.
- Outcome of NightDrive Eyewear's Kickstarter unknown.

## 8. Price sensitivity and positioning — what do customers pay, and is there a visible gap?

### Takeaway
The segment pays almost nothing for generic night glasses (€2–10), €34–50 for a branded one from a trusted channel (ANWB/Sinner), €50–70 for mainstream photochromic cycling glasses, €130–160+ for premium cycling eyewear and €44–71 for a replacement helmet visor — on top of €35–250 helmets and €3,000–10,000+ (mostly leased) speed pedelecs. The visible gap is a cycling/speed-pedelec-specific anti-glare product in the ~€30–70 band; the constraint is credibility of the claim, not willingness to pay.

### Cited Findings
- Generic night glasses: €1.99 at Action; a branded pair at €49.99 was judged by one ANWB reviewer as equivalent to "~€10" alternatives. — [Action BE](https://www.action.com/nl-be/p/3226374/fotochromatische-nachtbril/); [ANWB webshop](https://www.anwb.nl/webwinkel/p/89652/brandon-x-nachtbril-zonnebril-sinner)

- Mainstream cycling photochromic glasses: BBB Impress PH €49.95–69.99. — [12GO Biking](https://www.12gobiking.nl/bbb-bsh-58ph-impress-ph-fietsbril); [Mantel](https://www.mantel.com/en/bbb-cycling-impress-small-ph-2021-carry-over-fietsbril)

### Inferences
- Reference prices in the buyer's head: "a yellow night glasses costs €2–10" (Action/marketplaces) versus "a decent cycling glasses costs €50–70". A cyclist-specific product can plausibly anchor to the cycling-eyewear reference (€30–70) only if it looks and functions like cycling eyewear (wrap fit, helmet-compatible, anti-fog, fit-over or prescription option) rather than like a yellow driving glasses.
- Speed pedelec riders are the least price-sensitive sub-segment (expensive bike, mandatory helmet, daily long commute, often employer-financed bike) and are concentrated in Flanders; lease budgets sometimes include accessories, which could make B2B2C (lease companies, dealers) a channel — this was not verified.
- Visible gaps: (1) no product in BE/NL positioned for cyclists' headlight glare; (2) no anti-glare visor option from any helmet brand; (3) no speed-pedelec-specialist accessory webshop. Visible barriers: (1) €1.99 Action product and €10 marketplace clones cap what an undifferentiated yellow lens can charge; (2) expert/consumer-body consensus against yellow night glasses; (3) visor compatibility fragmentation; (4) strongly seasonal demand (roughly October–February).
- For the Netherlands the reachable premium niche is small (35,415 speed pedelecs, ~3,100 new per year, older private owners); the Dutch opportunity is the mass e-bike commuter, where price sensitivity is higher and Action/ANWB already serve the "nachtbril" need.

### Gaps
- No data on actual accessory spend per speed pedelec rider or on whether Belgian bike-lease contracts cover eyewear/helmet accessories.
- No conversion or sales-volume data for any nachtbril seller (review counts at ANWB — 25 and 45 — are the only volume proxy retrieved, and they are low).
- Willingness to pay for a cyclist-specific anti-glare product is untested; would need a landing-page/ads smoke test in October–January.

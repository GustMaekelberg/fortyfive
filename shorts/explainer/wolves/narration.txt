The internet says wolves reshaped Yellowstone, even changing the course of its rivers.
The data says: it's only half true.
Gone by 1926, wolves came from Canada: 14 in 1995, 17 in 1996.
Elk fell from 19,045 in 1994 to 3,915 in 2013, down 79 percent.
Ecologists call this a trophic cascade: predators thin herbivores, and plants recover.
Where browsing eased, willows grew taller, and beavers gained food and building material.
Beaver colonies did rise, from one in 1996 to twelve by 2009.
The viral story ends at rivers, but that is where the evidence thins.
Willows grew taller but covered no more ground, and incised streams limit recovery.
Hunting, drought and grizzlies also cut elk numbers, so wolves were not alone.
In 2025, 6,124 of 7,226 counted elk were outside the park's protection.
Wolves mattered, but the cascade was patchier and weaker than the legend says.

One DNA molecule becomes more than a billion copies in just thirty cycles.
Every cycle has three steps, and each step is just a temperature.
At 95 degrees Celsius, the double helix unzips into single strands.
At about 55 degrees, short primers latch onto their matching sequences.
At 72 degrees, a polymerase builds a new strand from each one.
Each cycle doubles the DNA, so thirty cycles give about 1.07 billion copies.
Normal enzymes die at 95 degrees, so early PCR re-added enzyme each cycle.
In 1969, Thomas Brock described Thermus aquaticus, a microbe from Yellowstone hot springs.
Its enzyme, Taq polymerase, survives the heat, so the machine only changes temperature.
Kary Mullis conceived PCR in 1983, and won the Nobel Prize in 1993.
Real reactions fall short of perfect doubling, so a billion is a ceiling.
Modern genetics leans on an enzyme from a Yellowstone hot spring.

Drug-resistant bacteria directly caused an estimated 1.27 million deaths worldwide in 2019.
Counting every death they contributed to, the toll reaches 4.95 million people.
In 1945, Alexander Fleming warned that underdosing would breed resistant microbes.
It is plain selection: the drug kills the weak and the resistant multiply.
Plasmids copy resistance genes from one bacterium to another, even across different species.
Biofilms add a slimy shield, making bacteria up to 1,000 times more tolerant.
Meanwhile, nearly all new antibiotics are tweaks of classes found by the 1980s.
WHO counts only 15 truly innovative drugs among 90 in clinical development.
The comeback may be phages, which are viruses that hunt bacteria.
A Belgian-led review of 100 personalised phage cases found 77 percent improved.
That was not a controlled trial, so the evidence is still fairly thin.
Resistance is not medicine failing; it is evolution simply doing its job.
